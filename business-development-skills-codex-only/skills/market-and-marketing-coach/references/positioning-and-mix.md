# Positioning and the marketing mix

Read this at Step 4 and use the result across Steps 5a–5d. Ground the analysis in the SWOT reversals and the alternatives and evidence from Step 3. Use the selected workshop language for all labels, interpretations, and tables.

## Choose three criteria
Select criteria that the intended user or payer can observe when choosing among alternatives. Define the low and high endpoints for each with concrete behaviors or quantities. Each criterion should convey different information. For example, for a simulated group conversation tool, compare control over social exposure, realism of conversational interaction, and support for moving into real group conversations. Define the last by observable features such as guided practice with real people or facilitated introductions. Treat actual transfer of skills as an outcome requiring evidence. Avoid vague axes such as general quality or innovation. When user and payer priorities differ, state whose decision each map represents and keep that perspective consistent across both.

## Draw and read both maps
- Give the criteria letters A, B, and C. Make **Map 1 A × B** and **Map 2 A × C**. State the purchase tradeoff each panel is meant to reveal. Use the same alternatives, units, axis direction, and range wherever an axis repeats. Use a stable visual marker for the venture and distinct markers for named alternatives.
- Assemble one evidence matrix before plotting. Record each offer's position and rationale on each criterion, with adjacent sources and evidence status. Use verified measurements if comparable. Otherwise use an anchored ordinal scale, e.g. low, medium, high, and mark each interpretive placement as a hypothesis. Keep unknowns unplaced on the relevant map and name them in its caption and matrix. Avoid invented decimal scores and treating display coordinates as measured gaps.
- Use readable axis captions, named labels or a legend, and compact captions explaining uncertainty. Where plausible scores tie, show the tie clearly. If the venture has no current offer, plot its **proposed** capability as such. Distinguish planned positions of existing offers from current ones.
- Explicitly name a lead and a gap where the matrix supports them, specifying criterion, comparator, and why the difference matters for the segment. If evidence cannot support either conclusion, state that and identify one useful check. Never engineer a lower placement just to show a gap or declare the venture best overall. Ask which advantage to protect and which gap materially affects the launch offer. Use one focused question when asking the student to prioritise.
- If a student changes a criterion, competitor, or placement, keep the matrix and both charts in sync. Reuse the resulting tradeoff in the 4 Ps discussion.

## Apply positioning to the marketing mix
Carry forward the same segment and the actual product advantage the venture can plausibly deliver. Connect each P to the segment's buying context and a relevant SWOT or positioning tradeoff. Keep Place about access/distribution and Promotion about communication. Check that the scope, price/payer, access route, and promotional promise can coexist. For example, a brand promising a private practice space may choose a product with controls over practice history, a price paid through a program, enrollment through an institution, and messaging that gives evidence of what privacy the product actually offers. Treat this as an illustration rather than a prescribed strategy.
